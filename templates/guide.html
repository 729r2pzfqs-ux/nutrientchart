{% if g.kind == 'dii' %}

Chronic low-grade inflammation is linked to heart disease, type 2 diabetes and some cancers, and diet is one of its modifiable drivers. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (Shivappa et al., 2014) scored 45 food components against six inflammatory markers across nearly 2,000 studies: fiber, magnesium, vitamins A, C, D and E, beta-carotene, omega-3 fats, folate, zinc and selenium score anti-inflammatory; saturated and trans fat, cholesterol and excess energy score pro-inflammatory. We apply those published weights to each food's USDA nutrient profile per 100 kcal, which is why vegetables, fruit, fish, legumes, nuts and seeds fill this list. Polyphenols, which the index also credits (tea, turmeric, berries, olive oil), are not in USDA's standard nutrient data, so this ranking understates those foods.

{% elif g.kind == 'el' %}

Electrolytes are the minerals that carry a charge in body fluids: potassium, magnesium, calcium and sodium above all. Most people get too much sodium and too little potassium and magnesium, so this list ranks whole foods by combined %DV of potassium, magnesium and calcium per serving (sodium counts for up to 10%). Sports drinks typically contain 100 to 300 mg of potassium per bottle; most foods below beat that.

{% elif g.kind == 'iron' %}

Plant (non-heme) iron is absorbed at roughly a third the rate of heme iron from meat, so vegetarians need more total iron and benefit from pairing it with vitamin C and keeping tea, coffee and calcium supplements away from iron-rich meals. The list below ranks plant and dairy foods by iron per serving and shows vitamin C alongside. Fortified cereals, which often carry 100% DV per serving, are excluded so the list reflects naturally occurring iron.

{% else %}

Nutrient density is how much nutrition a food delivers per calorie. The NRF9.3 index (Drewnowski, 2009) sums the % Daily Value of nine nutrients to encourage (protein, fiber, vitamins A, C and E, calcium, iron, potassium, magnesium), each capped at 100%, and subtracts three to limit (saturated fat, added sugar, sodium), all per 100 kcal. Leafy greens dominate any per-calorie measure; the table also shows calories per serving so you can judge absolute amounts.

{% endif %}
